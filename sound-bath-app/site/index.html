The line

One violin player becomes a symphony.

Thursday. A text lands — musicians you love are passing through town, and nothing is planned. Fifteen minutes at a laptop builds the structure: nine sections, two keys, a phrase carried across the arc, a floor of sound the room can stand on.

By Saturday there are sixty people on the floor in candlelight. Live bowls, a flute, chimes roaming the room. The plan runs itself, advances when you say go, and freezes when the room needs longer — so you spend the evening where you belong: present, in it, leading.

The promise isn't better sound baths. It's that you can say yes on Thursday.

How it holds

A structure, a sheet, and a room

The arc

Five questions become a runnable session: sections measured in minutes, a chakra choosing each key, a phrase carrying each section. Drag anything. The whole evening is one page.

The sheet

Every section computes a bowl line for the kit in the room — these bowls freely, those sparingly, that one rests, it fights the key. On your stand, and on any phone across the room, readable at fifteen feet.

The room

Tell it who's here and what they play — a roster, not a mixer. Someone roams with chimes and the app's motif yields. With live players, it plays only what humans physically can't: the floor, the drone, the air.

The engine keeps five laws
  • No pulse, ever. Nothing is scheduled to a tempo.
  • No third in the drone. It never decides major or minor for the room.
  • No automated dynamics. Levels change by arrangement, or by a human hand.
  • No competing melody. The motif is sparse, quiet, and yields to any melodic human.
  • Never bright by default. Every default errs dark. Tone only ever adds air.
