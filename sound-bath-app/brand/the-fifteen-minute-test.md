# The fifteen-minute test

2026-07-31. Adam, mid-thought, produced the thing this entire product should be
measured against:

> *"That should be our fucking test. Do a night where three different people
> create a 15-minute bath — and if they can all do it intuitively, in 15
> minutes they can set it up, and then everyone can stay on key and do it.
> I actually think it's doable, and that's life-changing."*

**Stop and take that seriously. It is a better test than anything else in this
folder**, because it is falsifiable, it can be run next month with people who
already exist, and it tests the actual claim rather than a proxy for it.

Everything else here is an argument. This is a measurement.

---

## What it actually tests

Bed's whole promise, stated as a single sentence you could win or lose:

> ### Someone who has never done sound design can build and lead a coherent session, and a group who has never rehearsed can play it together, in one evening.

Break that into the four things that each have to be true, because a failure
tells you *which one* broke:

| | The claim | What failure looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · Authoring** | a non-designer can build 15 minutes that holds | they stall, ask what a section is, or build something shapeless |
| **2 · Setup** | a room of mixed kits gets synced without a technician | someone can't join, someone's numbers are wrong, someone's tuning is off |
| **3 · Ensemble** | people who've never played together stay in one key | it turns to mud, or people stop trusting the sheet and just follow the loudest person |
| **4 · Leading** | the leader can be *present* rather than operating | they're head-down in the phone all night |

**Four is the one to watch hardest**, and it's the one nobody thinks to
measure. The product's real claim isn't "you can run a session" — it's *"you
can run a session and still be in the room."* Adam's own words for it:

> *"It becomes the instrument for executing all that really simply, and it
> allows you to do what you actually love — be present with the sound."*

---

## The protocol

An evening, six to ten people, three of whom lead. Roughly two and a half
hours.

1. **Everyone arrives with their bowls.** Stickers on, numbered low to high,
   scale checked going up. *(Fifteen minutes, done at the door.)*
2. **Three people each build a 15-minute session.** Same room, at the same
   time, on their own phones. **Nobody helps them.**
3. **Each of the three leads theirs**, with everyone else as the ensemble on
   the sheet.
4. **Fifteen minutes at the end**, everyone talking, while it's still fresh.

That's it. Don't add a workshop, don't add teaching, don't add a talk. Those
are [`the-workshop.md`](the-workshop.md), and they are a **different event with
a different purpose** — teaching people improves the outcome and destroys the
measurement.

---

## ⚠️ How this test gets accidentally rigged

The single most likely outcome is a lovely evening that proves nothing. Every
item below is a way to get a false pass, and all of them are tempting in the
moment.

- **⚠️ Adam helps.** He is a musician, he understands tuning systems, he built
  this, and he will *want* to lean over and fix things. **Every time he does,
  the test learns nothing.** Sit on your hands and write down what people got
  stuck on. Their confusion is the entire data set.
- **⚠️ Adam is one of the three leaders.** He can't be. He is the least
  representative person in the building.
- **⚠️ Only musicians in the room.** At least one of the three should be
  someone who leads baths but does *not* read music. That person is the
  customer.
- **⚠️ Pre-built sessions.** If anyone arrives having already made theirs, the
  authoring claim is untested — and authoring is what the subscription charges
  for.
- **⚠️ Judging it by whether it sounded good.** It probably will; a group of
  people playing in one key over a drone usually does. **That is not the
  question.** The question is whether people who'd never met could do it
  without a musician in the room.
- **⚠️ Fixing the app during the evening.** Write it down, ship it after.

> **The instinct that makes the night lovely is the same instinct that makes
> the result worthless. Pick one, in advance.**

---

## What to write down — and it isn't opinions

Ask nobody how they felt about the app until the very end. Record these
instead, because they're facts:

- **Minutes to a finished session**, per person. The claim is 15.
- **Every question asked out loud.** A question is a piece of the interface
  that isn't there. These are worth more than any praise.
- **Every moment someone looked up from their phone confused**, and what was
  on the screen.
- **How long from "scan this" to everyone connected** — and how many needed
  help.
- **Whether the leaders looked at the room or the screen.** Just watch. This
  is claim 4 and nobody will report it accurately about themselves.

Only then: *"what would have made that easier?"*

---

## What a pass and a fail each mean

**Pass — all three built something in ~15 minutes and the room stayed
together.** Then the product thesis is real, and the next question is
distribution, not design. That's a completely different company than the one
where you're still asking whether it works.

**Partial — the leading worked, the authoring didn't.** Most likely outcome,
honestly. It means the wizard is the product surface that matters and it isn't
finished. **That's a good result** — it's specific and it's fixable.

**Fail — the room couldn't stay together.** Then something in the chain from
kit → tuning → numbering → sheet is wrong in practice, and no amount of the
rest of this folder matters until it isn't. This is precisely why
[`numbering-and-tuning.md`](numbering-and-tuning.md) and
[`stickers.md`](stickers.md) exist: they are the three links most likely to
break here, and all three break *silently*.

## ⚠️ On "I don't know if there's a market outside Ohio"

Adam, honestly: *"I can already think of multiple people in my region I
legitimately think would use this. I don't know if anyone cares outside of the
people I know in Ohio."*

**Three straight answers.**

**1 · You cannot find that out by thinking, and you don't need to yet.** Ten to
twenty people who'd genuinely use it is not a market, but it is *exactly*
enough to run this test — and until the test passes, the size of the market is
not the binding question. Nothing about Ohio is special except that it's where
you can see.

**2 · The real risk isn't total market size. It's local density.** Sound bath
practice is global and growing; that part is not in doubt. But Bed's best
feature only pays off when **several practitioners in one town** adopt it —
one person alone gets a good instrument, three people in a room get something
nobody else has. So the honest risk is not *"does anyone want this"* but
**"is any given city thick enough."**

That's a much more useful thing to be worried about, because it prescribes the
strategy: **go city by city, led by a practitioner, not by advertising.** Which
is what [`the-workshop.md`](the-workshop.md) and
[`affiliates.md`](affiliates.md) already describe. Ohio isn't a limitation
here — it's the template.

**3 · The big-production point is right, and it isn't a customer.** *"The big
ones use composers and producers — but even that is a good sign, because if you
shorten the gap between that person and the average person who loves sound, you
have something."* Exactly right, and the second half is the whole thesis. Just
don't chase the first half: a production with a composer on staff is not a
segment Bed wins, and designing for them would wreck the thing that makes it
work for everyone else.

## The thing this test would prove, if it passes

Worth writing down now, before the evening, so the claim can't drift
afterwards:

> **Today, running a layered, structured, multi-player sound bath requires
> someone who is simultaneously a sound designer, an arranger, and a bandleader.
> After Bed, it requires someone who loves sound and can hold a room.**

That's the sentence. If the evening goes well, it's true and it's provable. If
it doesn't, it needs to change — and the version that survives will be better
than the one written in advance.
