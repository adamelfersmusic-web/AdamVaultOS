Wavetable lab

what a producer's sound becomes inside Bed

Each of these is a harmonic spectrum — a list of amplitudes, a few hundred bytes — played through Bed's own Bed-lane chain: dark lowpass tracking pitch, slow filter breath, stereo spread, the big dark reverb. This is what a curator pack would actually ship.

Pick one and press play. Switching while it plays crossfades over 4 s.

What to listen for. These all play the same notes — root, fifth, octave, ninth, never a third. The pack changes the timbre, never the harmony. A producer designs the colour; Bed still decides the voicing.

Why the choir is different. Every other entry here is a wavetable — a spectrum, a few hundred bytes. The choir is architecture: eight singers a few cents apart through fixed formant filters, plus breath. Vowels are fixed frequencies and wavetable harmonics travel with the note, so a choir built as a spectrum would chipmunk as the key migrates. Some sounds ship as data; some need code. Worth knowing before commissioning one.

The honest limit. A single spectrum is static — it can't evolve, and it carries no attack, no effects, no performance. Bed's filter breath supplies the only motion. If producer packs are worth doing, the extension that makes them expressive is morphing between two or three spectra over 30–60 s — lawful, because that's timbral drift rather than automated dynamics.

Everything here obeys the five laws: no pulse, no third, no automated dynamics, nothing bright by default. Nothing is sampled — every one of these is arithmetic.
